\section{Comparison of the three local routes}
\label{sec:route-comparison}

The endpoint, interval, and \(G_2\) sections introduce three ways to place the DeVries determinant inside a theory with localized gauge and order-parameter data.  This section puts them in a common format.  The purpose is to make the next analytical work precise: each route must deliver the same normalized two-channel kernel, the same electroweak assignment, the same branch interpretation, and compatible global data.  The comparison also prevents conceptual drift.  A successful route supplies a physical origin for entries of a matrix; it gives more than an analogy with strings, extra dimensions, or singular geometry.

\subsection{Common reduction problem}
\label{sec:common-reduction-problem}

Let \(\mathcal S_r\) denote one of the three route descriptions,
\begin{equation}
  r\in
  \{\mathrm{end},\mathrm{int},G_2\},
  \qquad
  \mathcal S_r
  =
  \begin{cases}
  \mathcal S_{\rm end} & \hbox{open-string endpoint or D-brane sector},\\
  \mathcal S_{\rm int} & \hbox{interval Kaluza--Klein boundary sector},\\
  \mathcal S_{G_2} & \hbox{localized singularity sector}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:route-source-list}
\end{equation}
The desired reduction chooses a two-dimensional light subspace
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H_{J,r}=\operatorname{span}\{h_{J,r},a_{J,r}\},
  \label{eq:route-light-space}
\end{equation}
where \(h_{J,r}\) is the order-parameter channel and \(a_{J,r}\) is the adjoint/current channel.  In this basis the reduced spectral kernel has the form
\begin{equation}
  K_{J,r}(\lambda)=
  \begin{pmatrix}
  \lambda+\Sigma_{hh,J}^{r}(\lambda) &
  \Sigma_{ha,J}^{r}(\lambda)\\
  \Sigma_{ah,J}^{r}(\lambda) &
  \lambda+\Sigma_{aa,J}^{r}(\lambda)
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:route-common-kernel}
\end{equation}
The route passes the first DeVries test when a stated limit and normalization give
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma_{hh,J}^{r}=0,\qquad
  \Sigma_{aa,J}^{r}=J,\qquad
  \Sigma_{ha,J}^{r}\Sigma_{ah,J}^{r}=J.
  \label{eq:route-common-target}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:route-common-target} is stronger than a qualitative mechanism.  It assigns one geometric, endpoint, or boundary object to each matrix entry.  It also fixes the algebraic route to
\begin{equation}
  \det K_{J,r}(\lambda)=\lambda^2+J\lambda-J,
  \qquad
  \lambda\in\{x_+(J),x_-(J)\}.
  \label{eq:route-common-det}
\end{equation}

The reduction has three addresses.  The endpoint address is supported by the open-string fact that endpoint labels and coincident branes generate matrix-valued gauge fields, with brane positions supplying scalar data \cite{TongString,Polchinski1994}.  The interval address is supported by extra-dimensional models in which boundary conditions and brane-localized terms set vector spectra \cite{CsakiHubiszMeade2005}.  The \(G_2\) address is supported by singular compactifications that localize gauge sectors and chiral matter, together with Higgs-bundle variables organizing local matter and interaction data \cite{WittenG2Anomaly2001,AcharyaWitten2001,AcharyaGukov2004,BraunEtAl2019}.  These sources justify the addresses.  They leave Eq.~\eqref{eq:route-common-target} as the shared derivation target.

\subsection{Entry-by-entry comparison}
\label{sec:entry-comparison}

The first comparison concerns the diagonal current entry.  In the DeVries matrix the current channel carries the trace contribution.  The route-specific question is therefore:
\begin{equation}
  a_{J,r}
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \Sigma_{aa,J}^{r}=J.
  \label{eq:route-current-question}
\end{equation}
For endpoints, \(a_{J,\rm end}\) should be a Chan--Paton or brane gauge amplitude.  The current trace would then come from a normalized endpoint algebra or an adjoint current on a coincident-brane stack.  For intervals, \(a_{J,\rm int}\) should be the boundary value of a bulk gauge mode or a current coupled to it; the trace contribution would come from the boundary kinetic or mass term after solving the bulk equation.  For \(G_2\), \(a_{J,G_2}\) should be a fluctuation along a singular gauge locus, with \(J\) arising from an ADE or adjoint invariant in the local gauge sector.

The second comparison concerns the off-diagonal product.  The determinant requires a product of transition amplitudes,
\begin{equation}
  h_{J,r}
  \leftrightarrow
  a_{J,r},
  \qquad
  \Sigma_{ha,J}^{r}\Sigma_{ah,J}^{r}=J.
  \label{eq:route-mixing-question}
\end{equation}
For endpoints, this product should arise from an open string stretched between sectors, a brane-Higgsing field, or an endpoint overlap.  For intervals, it should arise from boundary matching between a localized order parameter and a bulk gauge profile.  For \(G_2\), it should arise from a localized matter wavefunction, flow-line overlap, bifundamental vev, or deformation coupling at a singular point.  The route earns physical content when the product is computed from normalized wavefunctions, endpoint algebra, or local geometry.

The third comparison concerns the order-parameter reference entry,
\begin{equation}
  h_{J,r}
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \Sigma_{hh,J}^{r}=0.
  \label{eq:route-h-reference}
\end{equation}
The zero entry is a normalization and symmetry statement.  In an endpoint model, it may come from choosing the boundary order-parameter amplitude as the reference channel.  In an interval model, it may come from a boundary condition or brane term that subtracts the local order-parameter self-energy at the pole.  In a \(G_2\) model, it may come from the critical-point normalization of the localized mode.  A derivation must name the symmetry, subtraction, or normalization that gives this reference value.

The following table records the three entries in a format useful for a referee.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.18\linewidth}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.24\linewidth}}
\toprule
Kernel entry & Endpoint/D-brane route & Interval KK route & \(G_2\) route\\
\midrule
\(\Sigma_{aa}=J\) & adjoint Chan--Paton current or coincident-brane gauge amplitude & boundary value of a bulk gauge mode with brane kinetic or mass data & ADE singular gauge fluctuation or local adjoint invariant\\
\(\Sigma_{ha}\Sigma_{ah}=J\) & stretched string, endpoint overlap, or brane-Higgsing field & localized order-parameter coupling to a bulk profile & matter wavefunction overlap, flow line, bifundamental vev, or singular deformation\\
\(\Sigma_{hh}=0\) & boundary reference channel for the order parameter & normalized boundary order-parameter self-energy & critical-point normalization for localized matter\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Electroweak assignment as a route-independent target}
\label{sec:route-ew-assignment}

The same electroweak assignment target applies to every route.  The low-energy field-theory data contain an \(SU(2)_L\) Higgs doublet and adjoint gauge currents.  Their natural quadratic invariants give
\begin{equation}
  J_H=\frac34,\qquad J_{\rm adj}=2.
  \label{eq:route-j-invariants}
\end{equation}
The target map is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_r:
  (\mathcal S_r,\mathcal H_{J,r})
  \longrightarrow
  (J_W,J_Z)=(J_H,J_{\rm adj}).
  \label{eq:route-assignment-map}
\end{equation}
This map is a theorem target because the observed W and Z vector bosons are both gauge-sector particles after symmetry breaking.  The route must explain why the charged comparison samples the order-parameter invariant while the neutral comparison samples the adjoint/current invariant.  A useful route therefore produces the ordered pair from the gauge-Higgs coupling structure, from endpoint boundary labels attached to the Higgsing field, from interval boundary data that distinguish charged and neutral sectors, or from a localized singularity model where doublet and adjoint data meet.

The pole-ratio reading then has the common form
\begin{equation}
  \frac{M_{W,\rm pole}^2}{M_{Z,\rm pole}^2}
  =
  \frac{x_+(J_H)}{x_+(J_{\rm adj})}
  \label{eq:route-pole-ratio-target}
\end{equation}
after the pole scheme of Sec.~\ref{sec:pole} has been fixed.  This equation states the desired low-energy placement.  A route that naturally yields the same quotient at a compactification scale or unification scale would still be useful, because it would locate the algebra in a source theory.  The pole reading would then require an additional matching theorem.

\subsection{Branch interpretation and scalar data}
\label{sec:route-branch-interpretation}

Every route diagonalizes the same two-channel kernel.  Therefore each route has to account for both eigenvectors.  Let
\begin{equation}
  u_{\pm,J,r}=
  \alpha_{\pm,J,r}h_{J,r}
  +
  \beta_{\pm,J,r}a_{J,r}
  \label{eq:route-eigenvectors}
\end{equation}
be normalized eigenvectors of the route kernel in the DeVries limit.  The positive branch supplies the pole-ratio clue.  The negative branch supplies the scalar-side question,
\begin{equation}
  u_{-,J,r}
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \mathcal F_{{\rm sc},r}.
  \label{eq:route-scalar-map}
\end{equation}
The functional \(\mathcal F_{{\rm sc},r}\) depends on the route.  In the endpoint route it may be a brane separation, a stretched-string scalar, or a brane-Higgsing vev.  In the interval route it may be a boundary order-parameter functional, a radion-like deformation, or a localized scalar eigenmode.  In the \(G_2\) route it may be a singularity-deformation parameter, a critical value of \(f_Q\), a charge-density functional \(\rho_Q\), or a localized modulus.

The scalar map has to be tied to the electroweak ray.  The gauge-Higgs system has a radial vacuum scale and a projective vector-mass ratio.  A route that derives the determinant should identify how the positive branch fixes the projective vector datum and how the negative branch enters the radial or scalar sector.  This is the point where the two-root structure becomes physically restrictive.  A determinant used for the pole clue automatically supplies its partner branch, and the route must assign that partner branch a controlled role.

\subsection{Scale-placement ledger}
\label{sec:route-scale-ledger}

The route comparison separates three scale placements:
\begin{equation}
  \hbox{pole scale},
  \qquad
  \hbox{compactification or boundary scale},
  \qquad
  \hbox{unification scale}.
  \label{eq:route-scale-placements}
\end{equation}
The manuscript presently takes the pole scale as the proposed attachment point for the electroweak clue.  This choice gives the project its strongest phenomenological motivation.  The GUT lineage supplies the comparison class: a clean weak-angle value can be a high-scale structural datum, then acquire low-energy meaning through running and thresholds.  In the present construction the clean value is attached to the pole ratio, so the scale-placement theorem is logically prior to any compactification geometry.

For endpoint and interval routes, the most direct challenge is the relation between a boundary determinant and a four-dimensional pole.  Boundary conditions usually define a spectrum before loop corrections and before the conversion to pole observables.  The route must explain how the determinant survives matching to the pole definition, or how the pole reading emerges from a dressed boundary self-energy.

For the \(G_2\) route, the challenge is sharper.  A local singularity model naturally lives at a compactification or high scale.  The local kernel can organize representations and couplings, yet a pole-spectrum reading requires a low-energy matching chain.  The useful outcome is therefore a two-stage theorem:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal S_{G_2}
  \longrightarrow
  K_{J,G_2}
  \longrightarrow
  \Delta^{-1}_{V,T}(s)
  \quad\hbox{with}\quad
  s=s_{W,Z}.
  \label{eq:g2-two-stage-pole}
\end{equation}
The first arrow is local geometry.  The second arrow is four-dimensional matching.  The current draft states both as open obligations.

\subsection{Referee decision tree}
\label{sec:route-decision-tree}

The comparison can be used as a decision tree for the next research pass:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose one source theory \(\mathcal S_r\) and one light subspace \(\mathcal H_{J,r}\).
\item Derive the reduced kernel \(K_{J,r}(\lambda)\) from an action, boundary condition, endpoint algebra, or local \(G_2\) operator.
\item Identify the normalization giving Eq.~\eqref{eq:route-common-target}.
\item Derive the ordered electroweak map in Eq.~\eqref{eq:route-assignment-map}.
\item Match the positive branch to the pole observable or state the matching theorem required for that placement.
\item Map the negative branch to a scalar, modulus, brane, or deformation functional.
\item Check global-form, anomaly, and representation compatibility.
\end{enumerate}
Each step has a defined failure mode.  Extra light channels enlarge the determinant.  A different trace or mixing product gives a different secular relation.  A high-scale placement changes the interpretation of the weak-angle clue.  An incompatible global form rejects a proposed compactification.  These outcomes would still be useful research results because they would classify the DeVries relation inside a larger family of spectral constructions.
